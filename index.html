Why this site exists
Keep UP Diverter-Free

This website is intended to help University Park residents understand how traffic diverters will affect their daily lives, review public information, and more meaningfully participate in decisions affecting our neighborhood streets.

Our position

Safer Streets. A Connected Neighborhood.

These goals do not have to be in conflict. Residents should understand the options, the data, and the potential consequences before major street changes are implemented.

University Park can pursue effective traffic-safety solutions without unnecessarily limiting access to our neighborhood. We support transparent, data-driven decisions and meaningful opportunities for residents to be heard.

🗣️

Meaningful public input

Residents should have clear opportunities to understand proposals, ask questions, and share their perspectives before major changes move forward.

📊

Data-driven decisions

Traffic interventions should be evaluated using clear data, stated goals, and measurable results—not assumptions about how drivers might behave.

🔄

Consider all impacts

A change that improves one intersection may affect nearby streets, residents, schools, businesses, and daily travel. Those effects deserve careful consideration.

Why we oppose diverters

A connected town deserves a thoughtful traffic-safety plan.

We support safer streets. We oppose using permanent traffic diverters when the costs, effectiveness, alternatives, and consequences for University Park residents have not been fully demonstrated.

1

More than a minor inconvenience, diverters push real, measurable costs onto many residents.

Some may argue that it is neighborly to spend “a couple of extra minutes” on a driving route. But for people who commute on these routes every day, those extra minutes add up to unacceptable negative impacts.

We drove the route. Diverting traffic at the Clagett-Pineway intersection to eliminate the straight-line commute between Queens Chapel and Adelphi adds approximately 2.5–3 minutes one way, or 5–6 minutes round trip, based on the route tested.

5–6 min. additional round-trip time
25–30 min. additional time each 5-day week
~22–26 hrs. additional driving over 260 workdays

Over a five-day work week, that is roughly a half-hour of additional commuting time. Over a month, it can approach two hours. Over a year of weekday commuting, the cumulative cost can become an entire day of life spent driving around our town.

And that is for one person making one daily round trip. Multiply that by the number of family members who use the route, and the costs become very clear—and unacceptable.

Pushing back against diverters is not being a bad neighbor; it is being protective of your family's valuable time and money.

Time estimates above use the stated 2.5–3 minute one-way route increase and a 260-workday year. Actual impacts will vary by driver, time of day, route, and traffic conditions.

2

People should drive on the roads that were designed to handle the traffic.

Clagett Road was designed to handle the traffic that flows from Adelphi to Queens Chapel. If you have ever gotten the impression that Woodberry is a narrower road than Clagett, you were right.

We measured. Clagett Road is approximately 1/2 foot wider than Woodberry. Moreover, Clagett only has parking on one side of the road. Both factors allow traffic in both directions to move more smoothly through Clagett.

Our roads were designed the way they were for a reason.
3

Diverters are a reactive, long-term solution to yesterday's temporary problem.

We all felt the pain of Purple Line construction at the intersection of Adelphi Road and Route 193. During construction, more cars than usual came through town.

But with that construction completed, even residents who live near the exits to Route 1 acknowledge that traffic coming through town has lessened.

Why pursue a dramatic redesign of town roads, with inconvenience and unintended consequences for many residents, to solve a problem that has already changed with the end of construction?

Before making a permanent change, we should make sure we are solving today's problem—not yesterday's.
4

Moving forward with diverters can become a sunk-cost fallacy.

The Town invested considerable money and time studying traffic from the end of 2024 into 2025. That money has been spent—and it cannot be recovered.

It is human nature to want something to come out of money already spent. But past spending should not determine today's decision if circumstances have materially changed.

The right question is not, “How do we make use of the study we already paid for?” The right question is, “Given what we know today, what solution best serves University Park?”

Past Town Councils and Mayors have also studied traffic and transportation issues in University Park. That history is useful context, but it should not create an obligation to pursue a solution simply because the issue has been studied before.

Good decision-making looks forward. Past spending should not dictate future policy.
5

Is it really cheaper?

A traffic diverter may look inexpensive when its price is compared only with the construction cost of another traffic-calming project. But the full cost of a transportation decision is broader.

Residents also bear the cost of additional driving time, additional fuel consumption, changed routes, potential congestion on neighboring streets, and the inconvenience of navigating around barriers every day.

Those costs deserve to be included when alternatives are compared.

A project is not truly “cheaper” if it simply shifts the costs onto hundreds of residents.
6

Diverters have different impacts depending on where you live.

A town-wide discussion should not treat every resident's travel experience as identical. A diverter at one connection can have very different consequences for residents in different parts of town.

Residents should be able to see how proposed changes affect common trips such as:

  • Getting from Queens Chapel toward Adelphi and Route 1.
  • Traveling between the northern and southern parts of town.
  • Getting to schools, churches, groceries, and other destinations.
  • Reaching Route 1 without being redirected onto narrower streets.
  • Returning home from work or errands during peak traffic.
Before approving diverters, show residents the route-by-route impacts for every ward—not just the expected traffic reduction at the intersection being changed.
7

We are not a gated community. Why are we trying to become a de facto one?

One of the stated goals of the traffic subcommittee is to “improve the atmosphere” of the Town.

We think a good atmosphere is a welcoming atmosphere—not one that sets up barriers to our neighbors in College Heights Estates, Calvert Hills, and beyond.

University Park is not a community of elitists. We welcome others. People from outside town come through University Park for many reasons: to attend University Park Elementary and Northwestern High, to worship, to shop for their families at Trader Joe's and Whole Foods, and to visit family and friends.

What does setting up barriers to our neighbors say about us as a town?
8

We do not actually know how the mapping algorithms will respond.

One of the main arguments for diverters is that adding time to the route through town will cause Waze, Google Maps, and other navigation apps to stop routing people through University Park.

That is an assumption. We do not control the algorithms, and we cannot force private mapping companies to stop routing drivers over public streets.

If adding a couple of minutes to a route through town still makes it shorter than alternatives—such as traveling from Adelphi to Route 410 and then up to Route 1—drivers may continue to use the route.

In other words, diverters may depend in part on a prediction about how navigation systems will behave. What is not a prediction is that residents forced to take longer routes will spend more time driving.

Ironically, making residents drive longer may increase traffic within town by putting more resident trips onto longer and potentially more complicated routes.

Facts, not feelings, must drive our decisions.
9

Traffic volume is not the same as unsafe driving.

Speeding, blowing through stop signs, tailgating, and other unsafe driver behaviors should be addressed with measures that target those behaviors.

Trying to keep “outsiders” out will not necessarily stop dangerous driving. It may, however, make residents spend longer on the roads.

It may also encourage drivers to choose less safe routes. For example, accessing Adelphi Road from Tennyson Road—where there is a curve and no traffic light—could become more attractive than using the safer exit at the light at Wells Parkway.

The Town's materials identify several traffic-calming approaches. Residents should be allowed to weigh their costs, safety benefits, and unintended impacts.

Target dangerous behavior directly instead of assuming that restricting access will make streets safer.
10

Diverters divide our town—in more ways than one.

We have heard a “fairness” argument: because Queens Chapel was closed, residents at other exit points in town should be allowed to limit access to their own roads as well.

But Queens Chapel Road bisects the town. Closing it had the effect of allowing residents to cross from one side of town to the other more easily and more safely.

Diverters do the opposite. They can turn straightforward daily routes into complicated trips around barriers.

A connected town should not solve one connection problem by creating a maze of disconnected routes elsewhere.
11

Residents deserve clear opportunities to understand proposals and share their perspectives.

Recent listserv discussions do not tell the whole story, but they provide a picture of significant concern about proposed diverters. We have seen an underlying theme of surprise and dismay at the proposals, along with a clear desire to give input.

A circulating petition against diverters has already gathered several hundred signatures.

Residents should not have to search through old meeting minutes or learn about major proposals by chance. We call on elected representatives to proactively solicit constituent feedback and make decisions in response to what the community wants.

Hear our voices.
Solutions worth evaluating

Safer streets do not require permanent diverters.

The Town's materials identify several traffic-control and calming approaches. Residents should be able to compare their costs, safety benefits, and unintended impacts before relying on permanent diverters.

Speed management

Use targeted speed humps, raised treatments, and other proven designs where speeding is documented rather than closing a key connection for all drivers.

Targeted signage and restrictions

Improve route guidance and test time-specific or location-specific controls where appropriate, with careful monitoring before making permanent changes.

Enforcement and safer intersections

Focus enforcement on documented stop-sign violations and speeding, and improve crossings and intersections for pedestrians and cyclists without severing neighborhood routes.

Put residents' routes first

What will a diverter mean for your daily route?

The impact of a diverter depends on where you live, where you are going, and which connections you normally use. Residents deserve to see those impacts clearly before a permanent change is made.

1

Route 1 / Adelphi access

How will residents traveling between University Park and Adelphi, Route 1, or nearby destinations be redirected?

2

Queens Chapel connections

What additional turns or travel time will be required for residents using Queens Chapel and nearby connections?

3

Woodberry and neighboring streets

Could traffic shift onto narrower residential streets or streets with different pedestrian and intersection conditions?

4

Cross-town trips

How will a diverter affect residents who need to travel from one part of University Park to another?

Questions worth asking

Good public policy starts with clear answers.

Ask your council member. Ask the Town. Ask for the data.

Will traffic simply move to another residential street?

When access is restricted at one location, residents should understand how traffic patterns may change elsewhere and how those effects will be measured.

What alternatives have been considered?

Residents should be able to compare diverters with other approaches such as traffic calming, intersection improvements, enforcement, signage, targeted restrictions, or other measures discussed in public materials.

How will success be defined?

Before implementation, the Town should clearly identify the safety and traffic outcomes it intends to achieve and explain how it will evaluate results.

What opportunities will residents have to comment?

Public meetings, committee discussions, agendas, minutes, and Council decisions should be easy for residents to find and follow.

What current data shows that diverters are necessary?

Residents should be able to review current traffic volumes, speeds, crash information, and other relevant measures—not simply historical conditions.

What happens if the diverters do not work?

Before installation, the Town should explain what metrics will be monitored, how long the trial will last, and what circumstances would cause the Town to modify or remove the treatment.

Where will displaced traffic go?

Residents should see before-and-after projections for surrounding streets, including streets that could receive additional traffic.

How will emergency response be affected?

Any permanent restriction should be evaluated for its effects on police, fire, ambulance, utility, and other emergency or service access.

How will schools, pedestrians, and cyclists be affected?

Traffic changes should be evaluated for their effects on walking, biking, school travel, crossings, and interactions between vehicles and vulnerable road users.

What will residents actually gain compared with the alternatives?

The Town should identify the measurable safety or quality-of-life benefit expected from diverters and compare that benefit directly with other available options.

Will residents have a meaningful opportunity to evaluate a pilot?

If a traffic-control treatment is considered, residents should know whether it can be tested temporarily, what data will be collected, and how the public will participate in evaluating the results.

“Before permanent changes reshape how residents move through University Park, the community deserves clear information, meaningful input, and a full discussion of alternatives.”
